About

How I got here.

I was born and raised in Kolkata. My path into chemistry began at Ramakrishna Mission Vidyamandira, Belur Math, and has since taken me through rubber technology, a period in industry, and doctoral work on electrocatalysis, to the electrified interfaces I study today in Toronto.

I completed a B.Sc. (Honours) in Industrial Chemistry at Ramakrishna Mission Vidyamandira and stayed on for an M.Sc. in Applied Chemistry with the University of Calcutta, growing copper phthalocyanine nanostructures by thermal evaporation. Two early experiences set the direction: a spell at the Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics studying how gold nanoparticles interact with proteins, and an Indian Academy of Sciences Summer Research Fellowship at INST Mohali making silicon quantum dots. Both taught me that a material is only interesting where its structure meets its function.

At IIT Kharagpur I took an M.Tech. in Rubber Technology with Prof. Anil K. Bhowmick, evaluating graphene as a multifunctional nanofiller in rubber compounds, partly alongside Birla Carbon. I then joined the Pulp and Fibre Innovation Centre at Grasim Industries, Aditya Birla Group, as a Research Associate. That experience provided an engineer’s education in dispersion, interfaces, specification and scale-up, and they left me with an instinct I still rely on: a material only matters once it survives contact with a real process.

I returned to research to work on the problem that had been bothering me — that the chemicals underpinning modern agriculture are still made with fossil heat. My doctoral work at Ramakrishna Mission Vidyamandira with Dr. Uttam Kumar Ghorai addressed the electrochemical fixation of N2 and CO2 on cobalt phthalocyanine catalysts: ambient, renewable-electricity-driven routes to ammonia, urea and nitric acid.

It became clear that experiments alone could not explain what we were seeing. A SERB Overseas Visiting Doctoral Fellowship took me to Prof. Samir H. Mushrif at the University of Alberta for a year, where computation moved from a supporting tool to the core of my research approach.

I am now a postdoctoral fellow with Prof. Chandra Veer Singh at the University of Toronto, building connected multiscale workflows — density functional theory, molecular dynamics with machine-learned interatomic potentials, multiphysics modelling and uncertainty-aware optimization — and extending them from catalysis into battery cathodes and solid-state electrolytes.

My broader aim is to understand structure–activity relationships well enough to design for them: to take a chemical question at the scale of a single active site and follow it honestly all the way to a working device, with every prediction meeting an experiment that can falsify it.

Research

Electrified interfaces by design.

Two of the largest problems in sustainable energy — making chemicals without fossil heat, and storing electricity safely at scale — are both decided at an electrified interface a few nanometres thick.

My doctoral work established that in electrocatalysis: ammonia, urea and nitric acid underpin the global nitrogen economy, yet their manufacture consumes roughly 2% of global energy and emits over 1% of anthropogenic CO2. Replacing that chemistry with ambient, renewable-electricity-driven synthesis is limited not by thermodynamics but by selectivity — the desired N–H, C–N and N–O bond-forming steps must outcompete a kinetically trivial hydrogen evolution reaction.

My current work extends the same framework to energy storage: cathode materials and their modification, solid-state electrolytes, and the electrode–electrolyte interfaces that govern capacity retention, ion transport and stability. The governing physics is shared — coordination environment, local field, ion and solvation structure, interfacial transport — so the same multiscale toolchain applies to both.

Across both, the method is constant: density functional theory, molecular dynamics with machine-learned interatomic potentials, and multiphysics modelling, connected to synthesis and electrochemical measurement so that every prediction meets an experiment.

Foundation · what the published work established

Coordination control at molecular active sites

Metal phthalocyanines gave a chemically exact platform: a single M–N4 centre whose ligand field and electronic structure can be tuned deliberately. A scalable route to hollow cobalt phthalocyanine nanotubes reduces N2 to NH3 at room temperature; 1D/2D coupling with carbon nitride then redirects selectivity across both NO3− and N2 reduction.

ACS Nano 2021, 15, 5230 ACS Sustain. Chem. Eng. 2023, 11, 6191 ACS Catalysis 2023, 13, 13516

Cooperative dual sites for C–N coupling

Electrochemical urea synthesis is the hardest selectivity problem in this space — simultaneous activation of two feedstocks, a C–N bond formed between short-lived intermediates, and suppression of three competing pathways. Dual-metal-site-mediated coupling was demonstrated and the site-selective co-reduction mechanism resolved on copper phthalocyanine nanotubes.

J. Mater. Chem. A 2023, 11, 13249 Adv. Funct. Mater. 2022, 32, 2200882 Mater. Chem. Front. 2023, 7, 3820 — invited review

The oxidative branch, and field control

Selective N2 oxidation to nitric acid was established by combining cobalt phthalocyanine with a carbon nitride surface — a rare demonstration that one molecular platform can be driven oxidatively. Two later studies made the local electric field an explicit, quantitative design variable, reconciling COMSOL field maps, DFT energetics and electrochemical measurement on the same geometry.

Multiscale and data-driven methodology

My current postdoctoral work develops connected DFT → MLIP molecular dynamics → continuum multiphysics → uncertainty-aware Bayesian optimization workflows for symmetry-broken 2D semiconductors, where an intrinsic out-of-plane dipole governs charge separation, defect behaviour and interfacial reactivity. The same toolchain now extends to storage: first-principles calculations resolved how crystal water sets Na-site access, Fe redox activity and electrolyte compatibility in Prussian blue cathodes for halide solid-state sodium-ion batteries.

The program

Three thrusts on one integrating platform.

01

Coordination- and defect-resolved design of cooperative active sites

HypothesisC–N coupling and multi-electron N-reduction require two chemically distinct, spatially adjacent sites whose binding energies are anti-correlated — a condition conventional scaling relations forbid on a single-metal surface.

Heteronuclear and axially-modified M–N4/M–Nx macrocycles anchored on 2D supports, dual-site alloys and high-entropy surfaces, and defect-engineered heterostructures. Periodic DFT/DFT+U with explicit solvation and constant-potential corrections resolves reaction networks, potential-determining steps, dissolution and segregation risk, and HER competition together — not as a ranking, but to extract interpretable descriptors a synthetic chemist can act on.

Validated reaction networksDescriptor design rulesOpen catalyst dataset
02

The electrified interface made explicit

HypothesisThe largest unexploited selectivity lever is not the catalyst but the electrolyte.

Static adsorption energies cannot describe an interface in motion. This thrust follows solvent, cations, anions and intermediates directly using classical and ab initio molecular dynamics, with MLIPs extending quantum-mechanical fidelity to the length and time scales the double layer actually requires — quantifying how cation identity and hydration, interfacial water orientation, local pH and hydrogen-bond networks set intermediate lifetimes and suppress HER.

Interfacial design mapsElectrolyte-selection protocolAtomistics → Faradaic efficiency
03

Symmetry-broken 2D architectures with built-in electric fields

HypothesisIf an external field improves selectivity, a field built into the lattice should do so permanently.

Janus and otherwise asymmetric 2D chalcogenides, polar heterostructures and molecularly-functionalised 2D supports carry an intrinsic out-of-plane dipole that pre-polarises the interface, drives directional charge separation, and shifts adsorbate binding without additional overpotential. This unites both research lines — testing whether internal fields achieve what nanostructure geometry achieved externally in the Cu2O and Ni(TCNQ)2 work.

Rigour and validation

Built to resist the field’s false-positive problem.

Nitrogen electrocatalysis has a documented false-positive problem. Standard practice in my work: quantitative 15N isotope labelling with NMR/MS confirmation, feed-gas purification, open-circuit and Ar-purged blanks, independent dual quantification by colorimetry and ion chromatography, and full reporting of yield-rate normalization. Computationally: documented convergence, explicit uncertainty, version-controlled workflows and published input structures.

Teaching

Connecting molecular insight to engineering judgment.

The most durable learning occurs when an equation stops being a symbol and becomes an explanation.

I structure teaching around a recurring cycle: predict, calculate, test, interpret. Students first articulate what should happen, then build a quantitative model, compare the model with evidence, and finally decide whether the result is physically credible.

As a teaching assistant in general chemistry laboratories, I learned that students often perform a procedure successfully without understanding why each step matters. I began using short pre-laboratory questions, live conceptual checks, and post-experiment interpretation to make the reasoning visible.

A typical class begins with a physical question rather than a finished derivation. Worked examples move from idealized systems to authentic research problems — electrochemical nitrogen reduction, carbon dioxide conversion, batteries, catalytic interfaces. In advanced courses, students reproduce a published result, challenge an assumption, quantify uncertainty, and propose a defensible extension.

Mentored to date: three undergraduate, two master’s and one international exchange researcher, contributing to one peer-reviewed publication and two conference presentations.

Signature elective

Electrochemical Reaction Engineering for Sustainable Synthesis

Lectures combined with a modelling studio. Students derive Butler–Volmer kinetics, model diffusion and migration, analyse impedance data, calculate selectivity and energy efficiency, and construct a 1D or 2D cell model. The final project evaluates a published electrosynthesis process from active-site mechanism to reactor performance — including uncertainty, safety, critical materials and scale-up constraints.
